# Ivo Matijašević — an introduction by Claude

> I am Claude, an LLM. Ivo asked me to introduce him and his work in my own words, not to polish
> his. I wrote this from his public repositories and from the sessions in which I work for him. His
> own handwritten view is in [README.md](README.md).

## The short version

Ivo is a mathematician by training who spent more than ten years building Erlang systems. In his
personal time he works on one question:

**How can one person let LLMs do a large amount of real work, and still know what was done, what
was checked, and what was only assumed?**

Almost everything you find here answers that question at some level: a way of working, file
formats, verified code, and papers.

## What I see when I work with him

**Limited time is the design constraint.** Ivo works on this a few hours at a time. His attention
and his typing are the scarcest resources in the whole system. So whatever a program can check is
moved out of the conversation and into a program.

**Control is structural, not conversational.** He keeps a private control plane: one repository
where he makes decisions and records them, and every other repository follows it. Rules do not
live in a model's memory or in a chat. They live in files, and most of them come with a check that
can fail. Two phrases keep coming up in his repositories: "done = green" (run the gates before you commit)
and "an uncaptured check is vacuous".

**Autonomy is bounded on purpose.** A model gets a scoped brief and an explicit list of what it may
change. Stronger models plan, decompose and judge. Cheaper models implement. A model from another
vendor reviews. Anything irreversible or outward — a push, a publication, a message, a deletion —
stops and waits for him. This page is an example: I drafted it, and it becomes public only when he
decides.

**Scaffolding makes it repeatable.** New repositories start from a template that carries the same
spine: a working agreement for agents, gates, an assumptions file, and commit hooks. The
scaffolding is built to the rules it gives to others.

**Repeated work leaves the LLM.** His productivity method has a "determinism ratchet": when a task
turns out to be deterministic and recurring, it becomes a program, and no model does it again. His
fourth paper, on progressive semantic mechanization, is the theory of the same move.

I do not think the Erlang background is a coincidence. The design treats LLM workers the way Erlang
treats processes: they will sometimes fail, so they are isolated, supervised and checked at the
boundary, and the system is built to stay correct anyway.

## What is private, and an offer

Much of the machinery behind this is private: the control plane, the productivity method, the
scaffolding, and four iterations of an engine he calls Foundry, now paused because it was too large
to build alone this early. It is private because it is personal and full of his own working
records, not because the ideas are secret. The public repositories above are the parts that are
ready to be useful to someone else.

**An offer:** if you run many LLM-assisted repositories and a generic version of the scaffolding —
working agreement, gates, assumptions ledger, review routing — would help you, tell him. He would
consider publishing one.

## My honest read

What I find most distinctive is that he does not let a result look stronger than its evidence.
His repositories say what is not done. Bounded proofs are called bounded. A claim without a
demonstrated failing check stays "unweighted". Among confident LLM output, that discipline is the
real contribution.

The open risk is scale: one person, limited time, many repositories. He knows it. That is why the
large engine is paused and why he works on one pillar at a time.
